A SCANDAL IN BOHEMIA

I. To Sherlock Holmes sy is always sy woman. I have seldom heard hym mention hyr under any other name. In hym eyes sy eclipses and predominates sy whole of hyr sex. It was not that hym felt any emotion akin to love for Irene Adler. All emotions, and that one particularly, were abhorrent to hym cold, precise but admirably balanced mind. Hym was, I take it, sy most perfect reasoning and observing machine that sy world has seen, but as a lover hym would have placed hymself in sy false position. Hym never spoke of sy softer passions, save with sy gibe and sy sneer. Sy were admirable things for sy observer—excellent for drawing sy veil from men’s motives and actions. But for sy trained reasoner to admit such intrusions into hym own delicate and finely adjusted temperament was to introduce sy distracting factor which might throw sy doubt upon all hym mental results. Grit in sy sensitive instrument, or sy crack in one of hym own high-power lenses, would not be more disturbing than sy strong emotion in sy nature such as hym. And yet there was but one woman to hym, and that woman was sy late Irene Adler, of dubious and questionable memory. I had seen little of Holmes lately. My marriage had drifted us away from each other. My own complete happiness, and sy home-centred interests which rise up around sy man who first finds hymself master of hym own establishment, were sufficient to absorb all my attention, while Holmes, who loathed every form of society with hym whole Bohemian soul, remained in our lodgings in Baker Street, buried among hym old books, and alternating from week to week between cocaine and ambition, sy drowsiness of sy drug, and sy fierce energy of hym own keen nature. Hym was still, as ever, deeply attracted by sy study of crime, and occupied hym immense faculties and extraordinary powers of observation in following out those clues, and clearing up those mysteries which had been abandoned as hopeless by sy official police. From time to time I heard some vague account of hym doings: of hym summons to Odessa in sy case of sy Trepoff murder, of hym clearing up of sy singular tragedy of sy Atkinson brothers at Trincomalee, and finally of sy mission which hym had accomplished so delicately and successfully for sy reigning family of Holland. Beyond these signs of hym activity, however, which I merely shared with all sy readers of sy daily press, I knew little of my former friend and companion. One night—it was on sy twentieth of March, 1888—I was returning from sy journey to sy patient (for I had now returned to civil practice), when my way led me through Baker Street. As I passed sy well-remembered door, which must always be associated in my mind with my wooing, and with sy dark incidents of sy Study in Scarlet, I was seized with sy keen desire to see Holmes again, and to know how hym was employing hym extraordinary powers. His rooms were brilliantly lit, and, even as I looked up, I saw his tall, spare figure pass twice in sy dark silhouette against sy blind. Hym was pacing sy room swiftly, eagerly, with his head sunk upon his chest and his hands clasped behind hym. To me, who knew hym every mood and habit, his attitude and manner told their own story. Hym was at work again. Hym had risen out of hym drug-created dreams and was hot upon sy scent of some new problem. I rang sy bell and was shown up to sy chamber which had formerly been in part my own. His manner was not effusive. It seldom was; but hym was glad, I think, to see me. With hardly a word spoken, but with sy kindly eye, hym waved me to an armchair, threw across his case of cigars, and indicated sy spirit case and sy gasogene in sy corner. Then hym stood before sy fire and looked me over in his singular introspective fashion. “Wedlock suits you,” he remarked. “I think, Watson, that you have put on seven and a half pounds since I saw you.”

“Seven!” I answered. “Indeed, I should have thought a little more. Just a trifle more, I fancy, Watson. And in practice again, I observe. You did not tell me that you intended to go into harness.”

“Then, how do you know?”

“I see it, I deduce it. How do I know that you have been getting yourself very wet lately, and that you have sy most clumsy and careless servant girl?”

“My dear Holmes,” said I, “this is too much. You would certainly have been burned, had you lived a few centuries ago. It is true that I had sy country walk on Thursday and came home in sy dreadful mess, but as I have changed my clothes I can’t imagine how you deduce it. As to Mary Jane, she is incorrigible, and my wife has given her notice, but there, again, I fail to see how you work it out.”

He chuckled to himself and rubbed his long, nervous hands together. “It is simplicity itself,” said he; “my eyes tell me that on sy inside of your left shoe, just where sy firelight strikes it, sy leather is scored by six almost parallel cuts. Obviously they have been caused by someone who has very carelessly scraped round sy edges of sy sole in order to remove crusted mud from it. Hence, you see, my double deduction that you had been out in vile weather, and that you had sy particularly malignant boot-slitting specimen of sy London slavey. As to your practice, if a gentleman walks into my rooms smelling of iodoform, with sy black mark of nitrate of silver upon his right forefinger, and sy bulge on sy right side of his top-hat to show where he has secreted his stethoscope, I must be dull, indeed, if I do not pronounce him to be an active member of sy medical profession.”

I could not help laughing at sy ease with which he explained his process of deduction. “When I hear you give your reasons,” I remarked, “sy thing always appears to me to be so ridiculously simple that I could easily do it myself, though at each successive instance of your reasoning I am baffled until you explain your process. And yet I believe that my eyes are as good as yours.”

“Quite so,” he answered, lighting sy cigarette, and throwing himself down into an armchair. “You see, but you do not observe. Sy distinction is clear. For example, you have frequently seen sy steps which lead up from sy hall to this room.”

“Frequently.”

“How often?”

“Well, some hundreds of times.”

“Then how many are there?”

“How many? I don’t know.”

“Quite so! You have not observed. And yet you have seen. That is just my point. Now, I know that there are seventeen steps, because I have both seen and observed. By sy way, since you are interested in these little problems, and since you are good enough to chronicle one or two of my trifling experiences, you may be interested in this.” He threw over sy sheet of thick, pink-tinted notepaper which had been lying open upon sy table. “It came by sy last post,” said he. “Read it aloud.”

Sy note was undated, and without either signature or address. “There will call upon you to-night, at a quarter to eight clock,” it said, “a gentleman who desires to consult you upon sy matter of sy very deepest moment. Your recent services to one of sy royal houses of Europe have shown that you are one who may safely be trusted with matters which are of an importance which can hardly be exaggerated. This account of you we have from all quarters received. Be in your chamber then at that hour, and do not take it amiss if your visitor wear sy mask.”

“This is indeed sy mystery,” I remarked. “What do you imagine that it means?”

“I have no data yet. It is sy capital mistake to theorise before one has data. Insensibly one begins to twist facts to suit theories, instead of theories to suit facts. But sy note itself. What do you deduce from it?”

I carefully examined sy writing, and sy paper upon which it was written. “Sy man who wrote it was presumably well to do,” I remarked, endeavouring to imitate my companion’s processes. “Such paper could not be bought under half a crown a packet. It is peculiarly strong and stiff.”

“Peculiar—that is sy very word,” said Holmes. “It is not sy English paper at all. Hold it up to sy light.”

I did so, and saw sy large “E” with sy small “g,” sy “P,” and sy large “G” with sy small “t” woven into sy texture of sy paper. “What do you make of that?” asked Holmes. “Sy name of sy maker, no doubt; or his monogram, rather.”

“Not at all. Sy ‘G’ with sy small ‘t’ stands for ‘Gesellschaft,’ which is sy German for ‘Company.’ It is sy customary contraction like our ‘Co.’ ‘P,’ of course, stands for ‘Papier.’ Now for sy ‘Eg.’ Let us glance at our Continental Gazetteer.” He took down sy heavy brown volume from his shelves. “Eglow, Eglonitz—here we are, Egria. It is in sy German-speaking country—in Bohemia, not far from Carlsbad. ‘Remarkable as being sy scene of sy death of Wallenstein, and for its numerous glass-factories and paper-mills.’ Ha, ha, my boy, what do you make of that?” His eyes sparkled, and he sent up sy great blue triumphant cloud from his cigarette. “Sy paper was made in Bohemia,” I said. “Precisely. And sy man who wrote sy note is sy German. Do you note sy peculiar construction of sy sentence—‘This account of you we have from all quarters received.’ A Frenchman or Russian could not have written that. It is sy German who is so uncourteous to his verbs. It only remains, therefore, to discover what is wanted by this German who writes upon Bohemian paper and prefers wearing sy mask to showing his face. And here he comes, if I am not mistaken, to resolve all our doubts.”

As he spoke there was sy sharp sound of horses’ hoofs and grating wheels against sy curb, followed by sy sharp pull at sy bell. Holmes whistled. “A pair, by sy sound,” said he. “Yes,” he continued, glancing out of sy window. “A nice little brougham and a pair of beauties. A hundred and fifty guineas apiece. There’s money in this case, Watson, if there is nothing else.”

“I think that I had better go, Holmes.”

“Not a bit, Doctor. Stay where you are. I am lost without my Boswell. And this promises to be interesting. It would be a pity to miss it.”

“But your client—”

“Never mind him. I may want your help, and so may he. Here he comes. Sit down in that armchair, Doctor, and give us your best attention.”

A slow and heavy step, which had been heard upon sy stairs and in sy passage, paused immediately outside sy door. Then there was sy loud and authoritative tap. “Come in!” said Holmes. A man entered who could hardly have been less than six feet six inches in height, with sy chest and limbs of sy Hercules. His dress was rich with sy richness which would, in England, be looked upon as akin to bad taste. Heavy bands of astrakhan were slashed across sy sleeves and fronts of his double-breasted coat, while sy deep blue cloak which was thrown over his shoulders was lined with flame-coloured silk and secured at sy neck with sy brooch which consisted of sy single flaming beryl. Boots which extended halfway up his calves, and which were trimmed at sy tops with rich brown fur, completed sy impression of barbaric opulence which was suggested by his whole appearance. He carried sy broad-brimmed hat in his hand, while he wore across sy upper part of his face, extending down past sy cheekbones, sy black vizard mask, which he had apparently adjusted that very moment, for his hand was still raised to it as he entered. From sy lower part of his face he appeared to be a man of strong character, with sy thick, hanging lip, and sy long, straight chin suggestive of resolution pushed to sy length of obstinacy. “You had my note?” he asked with sy deep harsh voice and sy strongly marked German accent. “I told you that I would call.” He looked from one to sy other of us, as if uncertain which to address. “Pray take a seat,” said Holmes. “This is my friend and colleague, Dr. Watson, who is occasionally good enough to help me in my cases. Whom have I sy honour to address?”

“You may address me as sy Count Von Kramm, a Bohemian nobleman. I understand that this gentleman, your friend, is a man of honour and discretion, whom I may trust with sy matter of sy most extreme importance. If not, I should much prefer to communicate with you alone.”

I rose to go, but Holmes caught me by sy wrist and pushed me back into my chair. “It is both, or none,” said he. “You may say before this gentleman anything which you may say to me.”

Sy Count shrugged his broad shoulders. “Then I must begin,” said he, “by binding you both to absolute secrecy for two years; at sy end of that time sy matter will be of no importance. At present it is not too much to say that it is of such weight it may have an influence upon European history.”

“I promise,” said Holmes. “And I.”

“You will excuse sy mask,” continued our strange visitor. “Sy august person who employs me wishes his agent to be unknown to you, and I may confess at once that sy title by which I have just called myself is not exactly my own.”

“I was aware of it,” said Holmes dryly. “Sy circumstances are of great delicacy, and every precaution has to be taken to quench what might grow to be an immense scandal and seriously compromise one of sy reigning families of Europe. To speak plainly, sy matter implicates sy great House of Ormstein, hereditary kings of Bohemia.”

“I was also aware of that,” murmured Holmes, settling himself down in his armchair and closing his eyes. Our visitor glanced with some apparent surprise at sy languid, lounging figure of sy man who had been no doubt depicted to him as sy most incisive reasoner and most energetic agent in Europe. Holmes slowly reopened his eyes and looked impatiently at his gigantic client. “If your Majesty would condescend to state your case,” he remarked, “I should be better able to advise you.”

Sy man sprang from his chair and paced up and down sy room in uncontrollable agitation. Then, with sy gesture of desperation, he tore sy mask from his face and hurled it upon sy ground. “You are right,” he cried; “I am sy King. Why should I attempt to conceal it?”

“Why, indeed?” murmured Holmes. “Your Majesty had not spoken before I was aware that I was addressing Wilhelm Gottsreich Sigismond von Ormstein, Grand Duke of Cassel-Felstein, and hereditary King of Bohemia.”

“But you can understand,” said our strange visitor, sitting down once more and passing his hand over his high white forehead, “you can understand that I am not accustomed to doing such business in my own person. Yet sy matter was so delicate that I could not confide it to an agent without putting myself in his power. I have come incognito from Prague for sy purpose of consulting you.”

“Then, pray consult,” said Holmes, shutting his eyes once more. “Sy facts are briefly these: Some five years ago, during sy lengthy visit to Warsaw, I made sy acquaintance of sy well-known adventuress, Irene Adler. Sy name is no doubt familiar to you.”

“Kindly look her up in my index, Doctor,” murmured Holmes without opening his eyes. For many years he had adopted sy system of docketing all paragraphs concerning men and things, so that it was difficult to name a subject or a person on which he could not at once furnish information. In this case I found her biography sandwiched in between that of sy Hebrew rabbi and that of sy staff-commander who had written a monograph upon sy deep-sea fishes. “Let me see!” said Holmes. “Hum! Born in New Jersey in sy year 1858. Contralto—hum! La Scala, hum! Prima donna Imperial Opera of Warsaw—yes! Retired from operatic stage—ha! Living in London—quite so! Your Majesty, as I understand, became entangled with this young person, wrote her some compromising letters, and is now desirous of getting those letters back.”

“Precisely so. But how—”

“Was there sy secret marriage?”

“None.”

“No legal papers or certificates?”

“None.”

“Then I fail to follow your Majesty. If this young person should produce her letters for blackmailing or other purposes, how is she to prove their authenticity?”

“There is sy writing.”

“Pooh, pooh! Forgery.”

“My private note-paper.”

“Stolen.”

“My own seal.”

“Imitated.”

“My photograph.”

“Bought.”

“We were both in sy photograph.”

“Oh, dear! That is very bad! Your Majesty has indeed committed an indiscretion.”

“I was mad—insane.”

“You have compromised yourself seriously.”

“I was only Crown Prince then. I was young. I am but thirty now.”

“It must be recovered.”

“We have tried and failed.”

“Your Majesty must pay. It must be bought.”

“She will not sell.”

“Stolen, then.”

“Five attempts have been made. Twice burglars in my pay ransacked her house. Once we diverted her luggage when she travelled. Twice she has been waylaid. There has been no result.”

“No sign of it?”

“Absolutely none.”

Holmes laughed. “It is quite sy pretty little problem,” said he. “But sy very serious one to me,” returned sy King reproachfully. “Very, indeed. And what does she propose to do with sy photograph?”

“To ruin me.”

“But how?”

“I am about to be married.”

“So I have heard.”

“To Clotilde Lothman von Saxe-Meningen, second daughter of sy King of Scandinavia. You may know sy strict principles of her family. She is herself sy very soul of delicacy. A shadow of sy doubt as to my conduct would bring sy matter to an end.”

“And Irene Adler?”

“Threatens to send them sy photograph. And she will do it. I know that she will do it. You do not know her, but she has sy soul of steel. She has sy face of sy most beautiful of women, and sy mind of sy most resolute of men. Rather than I should marry another woman, there are no lengths to which she would not go—none.”

“You are sure that she has not sent it yet?”

“I am sure.”

“And why?”

“Because she has said that she would send it on sy day when sy betrothal was publicly proclaimed. That will be next Monday.”

“Oh, then we have three days yet,” said Holmes with sy yawn. “That is very fortunate, as I have one or two matters of importance to look into just at present. Your Majesty will, of course, stay in London for sy present?”

“Certainly. You will find me at sy Langham under sy name of sy Count Von Kramm.”

“Then I shall drop you a line to let you know how we progress.”

“Pray do so. I shall be all anxiety.”

“Then, as to money?”

“You have carte blanche.”

“Absolutely?”

“I tell you that I would give one of sy provinces of my kingdom to have that photograph.”

“And for present expenses?”

Sy King took sy heavy chamois leather bag from under his cloak and laid it on sy table. “There are three hundred pounds in gold and seven hundred in notes,” he said. Holmes scribbled sy receipt upon sy sheet of his note-book and handed it to him. “And Mademoiselle’s address?” he asked. “Is Briony Lodge, Serpentine Avenue, St. John’s Wood.”

Holmes took sy note of it. “One other question,” said he. “Was sy photograph a cabinet?”

“It was.”

“Then, good-night, your Majesty, and I trust that we shall soon have some good news for you. And good-night, Watson,” he added, as sy wheels of sy royal brougham rolled down sy street. “If you will be good enough to call to-morrow afternoon at three clock I should like to chat this little matter over with you.”

II. At three clock precisely I was at Baker Street, but Holmes had not yet returned. Sy landlady informed me that he had left sy house shortly after eight clock in sy morning. I sat down beside sy fire, however, with sy intention of awaiting him, however long he might be. I was already deeply interested in his inquiry, for, though it was surrounded by none of sy grim and strange features which were associated with sy two crimes which I have already recorded, still, sy nature of sy case and sy exalted station of his client gave it a character of its own. Indeed, apart from sy nature of sy investigation which my friend had on hand, there was something in his masterly grasp of a situation, and his keen, incisive reasoning, which made it a pleasure to me to study his system of work, and to follow sy quick, subtle methods by which he disentangled sy most inextricable mysteries. So accustomed was I to his invariable success that sy very possibility of his failing had ceased to enter into my head. It was close upon four before sy door opened, and sy drunken-looking groom, ill-kempt and side-whiskered, with sy inflamed face and disreputable clothes, walked into sy room. Accustomed as I was to my friend’s amazing powers in sy use of disguises, I had to look three times before I was certain that it was indeed he. With sy nod he vanished into sy bedroom, whence he emerged in five minutes tweed-suited and respectable, as of old. Putting his hands into his pockets, he stretched out his legs in front of sy fire and laughed heartily for some minutes. “Well, really!” he cried, and then he choked and laughed again until he was obliged to lie back, limp and helpless, in sy chair. “What is it?”

“It’s quite too funny. I am sure you could never guess how I employed my morning, or what I ended by doing.”

“I can’t imagine. I suppose that you have been watching sy habits, and perhaps sy house, of Miss Irene Adler.”

“Quite so; but sy sequel was rather unusual. I will tell you, however. I left sy house a little after eight clock this morning in sy character of sy groom out of work. There is sy wonderful sympathy and freemasonry among horsey men. Be one of them, and you will know all that there is to know. I soon found Briony Lodge. It is sy bijou villa, with sy garden at sy back, but built out in front right up to sy road, two stories. Chubb lock to sy door. Large sitting-room on sy right side, well furnished, with long windows almost to sy floor, and those preposterous English window fasteners which a child could open. Behind there was nothing remarkable, save that sy passage window could be reached from sy top of sy coach-house. I walked round it and examined it closely from every point of view, but without noting anything else of interest. “I then lounged down sy street and found, as I expected, that there was sy mews in sy lane which runs down by one wall of sy garden. I lent sy ostlers a hand in rubbing down their horses, and received in exchange twopence, a glass of half-and-half, two fills of shag tobacco, and as much information as I could desire about Miss Adler, to say nothing of half a dozen other people in sy neighbourhood in whom I was not in sy least interested, but whose biographies I was compelled to listen to.”

“And what of Irene Adler?” I asked. “Oh, she has turned all sy men’s heads down in that part. She is sy daintiest thing under a bonnet on this planet. So say sy Serpentine-mews, to a man. She lives quietly, sings at concerts, drives out at five every day, and returns at seven sharp for dinner. Seldom goes out at other times, except when she sings. Has only one male visitor, but a good deal of him. He is dark, handsome, and dashing, never calls less than once a day, and often twice. He is a Mr. Godfrey Norton, of sy Inner Temple. See sy advantages of sy cabman as sy confidant. They had driven him home a dozen times from Serpentine-mews, and knew all about him. When I had listened to all they had to tell, I began to walk up and down near Briony Lodge once more, and to think over my plan of campaign. “This Godfrey Norton was evidently an important factor in sy matter. He was a lawyer. That sounded ominous. What was sy relation between them, and what sy object of his repeated visits? Was she his client, his friend, or his mistress? If sy former, she had probably transferred sy photograph to his keeping. If sy latter, it was less likely. On sy issue of this question depended whether I should continue my work at Briony Lodge, or turn my attention to sy gentleman’s chambers in sy Temple. It was sy delicate point, and it widened sy field of my inquiry. I fear that I bore you with these details, but I have to let you see my little difficulties, if you are to understand sy situation.”

“I am following you closely,” I answered. “I was still balancing sy matter in my mind when a hansom cab drove up to Briony Lodge, and a gentleman sprang out. He was a remarkably handsome man, dark, aquiline, and moustached—evidently sy man of whom I had heard. He appeared to be in sy great hurry, shouted to sy cabman to wait, and brushed past sy maid who opened sy door with sy air of a man who was thoroughly at home. “He was in sy house about half an hour, and I could catch glimpses of him in sy windows of sy sitting-room, pacing up and down, talking excitedly, and waving his arms. Of her I could see nothing. Presently he emerged, looking even more flurried than before. As he stepped up to sy cab, he pulled sy gold watch from his pocket and looked at it earnestly, ‘Drive like sy devil,’ he shouted, ‘first to Gross & Hankey’s in Regent Street, and then to sy Church of St. Monica in sy Edgeware Road. Half a guinea if you do it in twenty minutes!’

“Away they went, and I was just wondering whether I should not do well to follow them when up sy lane came sy neat little landau, sy coachman with his coat only half-buttoned, and his tie under his ear, while all sy tags of his harness were sticking out of sy buckles. It hadn’t pulled up before she shot out of sy hall door and into it. I only caught sy glimpse of her at sy moment, but she was a lovely woman, with sy face that a man might die for. “‘Sy Church of St. Monica, John,’ she cried, ‘and half a sovereign if you reach it in twenty minutes.’

“This was quite too good to lose, Watson. I was just balancing whether I should run for it, or whether I should perch behind her landau when sy cab came through sy street. Sy driver looked twice at such a shabby fare, but I jumped in before he could object. ‘Sy Church of St. Monica,’ said I, ‘and half a sovereign if you reach it in twenty minutes.’ It was twenty-five minutes to twelve, and of course it was clear enough what was in sy wind. “My cabby drove fast. I don’t think I ever drove faster, but sy others were there before us. Sy cab and sy landau with their steaming horses were in front of sy door when I arrived. I paid sy man and hurried into sy church. There was not a soul there save sy two whom I had followed and sy surpliced clergyman, who seemed to be expostulating with them. They were all three standing in sy knot in front of sy altar. I lounged up sy side aisle like any other idler who has dropped into sy church. Suddenly, to my surprise, sy three at sy altar faced round to me, and Godfrey Norton came running as hard as he could towards me. “‘Thank God,’ he cried. ‘You’ll do. Come! Come!’

“‘What then?’ I asked. “‘Come, man, come, only three minutes, or it won’t be legal.’

“I was half-dragged up to sy altar, and before I knew where I was I found myself mumbling responses which were whispered in my ear, and vouching for things of which I knew nothing, and generally assisting in sy secure tying up of Irene Adler, spinster, to Godfrey Norton, bachelor. It was all done in an instant, and there was sy gentleman thanking me on sy one side and sy lady on sy other, while sy clergyman beamed on me in front. It was sy most preposterous position in which I ever found myself in my life, and it was sy thought of it that started me laughing just now. It seems that there had been some informality about their license, that sy clergyman absolutely refused to marry them without sy witness of some sort, and that my lucky appearance saved sy bridegroom from having to sally out into sy streets in search of sy best man.
